Mechanism of Action

4.1 More Foam and Suds Equal Better Cleaning

Excess foam is detrimental to cleaning performance, especially in modern high-efficiency (HE) washing machines. High-quality detergents are engineered to be low-sudsing.

Modern HE washers use significantly less water than traditional top-loaders. They are designed to clean by repeatedly lifting clothes out of the water and dropping them back in, maximizing mechanical action between the fabrics. A thick layer of foam creates a cushioning effect, preventing this fabric-on-fabric agitation and reducing cleaning performance. Furthermore, excessive suds can be difficult to rinse away, leading to residue build-up on both clothes and the machine itself. Therefore, high-quality HE detergents are intentionally formulated with low-sudsing surfactant systems to ensure optimal mechanical action and a clean rinse.

4.2 A Strong Perfume Scent Equals a Cleaner Garment

Heavy fragrance can be used to mask residual malodor that a detergent failed to remove. A truly high-quality clean is defined by the absence of malodor at its source, not the presence of a strong perfume.

As established in Section 2, malodor originates from the breakdown of residual sebum and microbial activity (Miracle et al., 2020; Lam et al., 2023). A high-quality detergent eliminates odor by using a system of surfactants, enzymes, and chelants to remove these precursors. In contrast, lower-performing detergents that fail to remove these root causes may use a high concentration of fragrance simply to cover up the underlying musty or sour notes. Blinded, in-home use studies have confirmed that consumers can differentiate between a true, deep clean and a perfumed mask, consistently rating the odor-free garments as higher quality (Cortez et al., 2024).

4.3 Only Hot Water Can Truly Hygienically Clean Clothes

This is an outdated concept from a previous era of detergent technology. Modern, high-quality detergents are specifically engineered with advanced enzyme systems to deliver superior cleaning and hygiene even in cold water (60-80°F).

The efficacy of hot water washing was based on thermal energy helping to melt greasy soils and providing some thermal disinfection. Today, this function is performed more effectively and sustainably by specialized ingredients. Advanced surfactants or lipase enzymes are highly effective at breaking down greasy soils at low temperatures (Obendorf et al., 2001; Hasan et al., 2010), while cold-adapted proteases have been engineered through biotechnology to have peak activity in cold water (Tindbaek et al., 2004). This multi-enzyme system allows modern detergents to achieve a deep, hygienic clean at 30°C that is similar to older detergents run at 60°C, while using up to 90% less energy and being gentler on fabrics.

Water Solubility

Detergent-grade PVA has been scientifically confirmed to be:

  • Water-soluble in laundry and dishwashing conditions[1, 2]
  • Designed in a way to maximize the solubility of the PVA[2, 3]
  • Water-soluble[2] at levels 50X higher than you would find in a dishwasher or laundry machine, using the international standard for polymer solubility [4]

Detergent grade PVA is so soluble that it dissolves down to the individual molecules, which do not and cannot reform into particles. This means there is no solid surface for toxic substances in the water to adsorb onto. [5, 6, 7]

Microplastics are insoluble [32]. Even the smallest possible fragments are collections of multiple molecules which do not dissolve.

This means they can accumulate in bodies of water. This means they have a solid surface to adsorb and transport other toxic chemicals. [8, 9, 10]

Biodegradability

Detergent-grade PVA degrades during the normal wastewater treatment process, using universally recognized standard testing methodologies (OECD 301B, 302B). [11, 12, 13]

It breaks down consistently and reproducibly across a range of laboratory sites, geographical locations, and under a variety of testing conditions, even in river water. [14]

Microplastics are extremely persistent in the environment, with degradation timelines ranging from decades to centuries, depending on conditions [10].

Presence in Water Bodies

Zero evidence of detergent-grade PVA has been found in the aquatic environment, including zero presence found in drinking water [15].

Microplastics have been detected in oceans, rivers, lakes, fish, birds, plankton, and remote areas, and in drinking water [10, 15, 16, 17, 18].

Accumulation in Living Species

It is not considered a risk, as it does not accumulate. Detergent-grade PVA has never been found in the human body [19, 20, 21, 30, 31].

Microplastics will accumulate due to their solubility in cells’ fat structures and have been found in most parts of the human body [10, 21].

Presence in the Home Environment

Detergent-grade PVA has never been found in the home environment, even in dishwasher and washing machine use or homes [1, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26].

Microplastics have been found in numerous household products as solid particles [1, 22, 23, 25, 26].

Toxicity

The environmental safety and human safety of detergent-grade PVA has been confirmed by the EPA, FDA, and other agencies around the world – and it is approved for use in things like detergent pods, eye drops, and the coating of medications [19, 20, 21, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31].

Microplastics can be harmful to marine life and pose a physical hazard to animals. They can also absorb pollutants due to their solid surface [7, 8].

Generally Recognized as a Microplastic

No, detergent-grade PVA is not generally recognized by the scientific community as a microplastic [10, 32, 33].

Yes, all these materials fit the generally accepted definition and categorization of a microplastic [10, 32].
